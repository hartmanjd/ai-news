01 Hacker News

AI usage generates a fragile codebase

A former Meta engineer who was laid off in May says he believes his reluctance to fully embrace AI coding tools was a factor, and he now works at another large tech company where he generates 100% of his code with AI because it is expected. In the article, he argues that heavy AI use produces a fragile codebase in four ways: AI writes shallow tests that miss important cases, writes overly complex code, reduces human understanding of the codebase over time, and moves too fast for developers to review properly. He lists warning signs including rising incidents, slower recovery from outages, errors only users see, and a worse user experience. He concludes that high-quality software for humans still needs a human touch, and that products built mainly by AI will be fragile.

04 Hacker News

Fireworks AI security breach impacts Notion and their customers

Notion says a security breach at its vendor Fireworks AI may have affected fewer than 1% of Notion workspaces. Fireworks, an AI infrastructure platform, told Notion on November 6 that malicious actors may have accessed customer data, and confirmed the dataset on November 8. Notion found that the data included unattributable Notion AI chat transcripts used for its Insights feature, meaning the chats were not labeled with who wrote them or which workspace they came from, and contained no tool call results. Notion had already cut off all connections to Fireworks on November 3, deleted its data there, and stopped sending traffic to the vendor; the Insights feature is now disabled. Notion has contacted affected workspace owners, who can review the data inside their workspace, and it advises anyone who pasted sensitive information like passwords or API keys into Notion AI during this period to rotate them.

10 Ars Technica

What happens to mathematics when AI becames an oracle?

Applied mathematicians Steven Strogatz and Alex Townsend have written a new book, "Big Math: The Hidden Codes That Run Our World," about how computers now handle math problems too large for the human brain, such as those with billions of variables. Strogatz, a well-known math communicator and Cornell professor, was assigned to mentor Townsend and the two began lunching together, which led to the collaboration. The book argues that moving math outside our heads into tools is a 2,000-year-old idea, and that linear problems can scale up while large non-linear ones remain hard to solve with rigorous proof. It also covers real-world uses like gerrymandering, where mathematician Moon Duchin generated thousands of random, legally valid maps to show that Alabama could have drawn two districts with Black majority voters. The authors say the challenge of this century is letting our power grow "without losing our soul."
