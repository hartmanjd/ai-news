02 Hacker News

Fuck Your AI Safeguards

A senior product engineer wrote that he asked Anthropic's Claude Code tool to check his own product for easy security fixes, and it found a simple one-line issue. He says the tool then blocked him from applying the fix because of "AI safeguards," and that getting an exemption would require waiting several business days, giving up data retention protections, and submitting a passport or ID, with no guarantee of approval. He says this has happened before when he tried to test whether public exploit code could be used against his services, and that Claude told him to use an older model instead. He argues criminals don't need approval because they can use open-weight models without safeguards, so the restrictions mainly hurt people trying to defend their own systems. He ended up using an EU-hosted open-weight model, GLM 5.3, which confirmed the issue and helped fix and verify it.

03 Hacker News

LLM assisted reverse engineering

A longtime hardware and software hacker describes how he now uses large language models to speed up reverse engineering. He uses DeepSeek v4.1 Flash as his main AI, along with the free Ghidra disassembler connected through an MCP bridge, and sometimes radare2 or kuna. His process is to point an AI agent at a binary, have it identify the file type and platform, document findings in markdown files, and map the basic structure before making changes. When the agent gets stuck, he has it debug the binary, add logging probes, or write down hypotheses in a table and design experiments to prove or disprove them. He says this approach let him reverse engineer devices like the Alphasmart NEO, map camera protocols for Sony mirrorless cameras, and build custom firmware for synthesizers and an ebook reader—work he says would have taken years by hand.

04 Hacker News

Claude solves two open problems in the physics of black holes

Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, solved two open problems in theoretical physics about black holes and quantum gravity. In the first, it completed a complex calculation that physicists first set up in 2003 but never finished, showing that the sudden transition where a hot gas turns into a black hole is abrupt even when spacetime becomes blurry at tiny scales. In the second, it found the exact upper limit for the simplest version of a method called bootstrapping, which physicists use to rule out wrong theories of quantum gravity. The work was done by agents running copies of Claude in the background for about six weeks, and the results were checked by a physicist and formally verified in a computerized proof checker. Neither result describes our own universe directly or has practical applications, but both add to the long effort to understand how quantum mechanics and general relativity fit together.

These tech outages are giving Y2K, AI agent style

In the past two weeks, several major U.S. companies and agencies faced technical outages that disrupted everyday systems. Apple Pay, NASA's internal communications, Microsoft's Outlook, Teams, and Copilot, and the FAA's flight systems all went down, with the FAA grounding all flights for hours. 10 Ars Technica

Traumatized teens sue school, cops after Doritos bag triggers AI gun alert

Four Black high school students in Baltimore County, Maryland, are suing their school district, police officers, and the AI gun detection company Omnilert after a false alert led to an armed police response. In October 2025, the school's Omnilert system flagged one student holding a bag of Doritos as a possible gunman; officials canceled the alert within two minutes, but the school principal still texted a school resource officer to send police, who arrived with guns drawn, handcuffed the teens, and searched them. The lawsuit says the students' constitutional rights were violated and seeks to bar the district from using Omnilert until proper protocols are in place. A state inspector general's report found the principal had not been trained on the system and that the district's alert process was flawed. The case matters because it shows how a false AI alert, combined with human error, can put students in real danger, and it raises broader questions about how schools use AI for safety.
