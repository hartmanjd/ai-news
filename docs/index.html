01 Hacker News

Google SWE points 48 AI agents at NASA Lidar, finds unmapped Maya pyramid in 72h

A Google software engineer used 48 AI agents to analyze public NASA lidar data of about 1,200 km² of Mexican jungle, and the agents flagged a hilltop site with a plaza, an oval pyramid 8–12 meters tall, a raised courtyard and a possible water reservoir. The engineer, who says he is not an archaeologist, sent a report to Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH), which replied that it had no registered archaeological site at that location. The work took under 72 hours, from a brainstorm with Gemini, ChatGPT, Claude and Grok to INAH's reply. An update later said the site had already been mapped in Estrada-Belli et al. 2025, so the agents rediscovered it rather than discovered it; it simply was not yet in INAH's register. The case matters because it shows AI agents doing scientific survey work beyond coding and math, though the missed literature also shows they still need checking.

02 Hacker News

Apple has new Mac app coming to make running local AI models easy

Apple is working on a new Mac app called Harbor, found by leaker Aaron Perris, that turns a Mac into a private AI inference server. The app runs open-weight AI models locally on Apple silicon, so data stays on the user's hardware and nothing goes to the cloud. It can also connect multiple Macs over Thunderbolt 5 to pool their memory, letting them run larger models or handle more requests than one machine could alone. Harbor is meant to be set up in minutes without command-line work, and it handles downloading and managing models inside the app. It is currently in a private preview under Apple's Claris brand, with a public launch possibly coming in the weeks or months ahead. This matters because it would make running local AI on Macs much easier and could push Apple further into the local AI market.

03 Hacker News

Tracking Anthropic-discovered vulnerabilities

Seven months into Anthropic's Project Glasswing, VulnCheck has tracked 442 CVEs attributable to Anthropic, about 0.5% of all CVEs published this year, and only two of them have been exploited in the wild. Anthropic's own disclosure ledger lists 37,966 findings but only 217 marked fixed, and since September it added nearly 12,000 findings while recording just 15 more fixes. About 1,654 findings (4.4%) are marked duplicates and 419 have been withdrawn, even as Anthropic's claimed true positive rate rose from 90.8% to 94.2%. Anthropic has shifted validation work to open source maintainers through its OSS Scanner, which produces reports without human review. The curl project received 45 reports and has published 34 so far: 19 were marked not a vulnerability, 6 were confirmed low severity, and 9 await assessment. The gap between reported findings and confirmed fixes matters because it tests whether AI vulnerability discovery claims hold up in practice.

04 Hacker News

Gemini Decisions API

Google has introduced the Gemini Decisions API, a new endpoint powered by its Gemini Decision 1 model, available through the Gemini API in Google AI Studio. The API is built for closed-set decisions like tool selection, policy checks, model routing, and context grading, and it can take one multimodal input across text, images, audio, video, and PDFs (up to 256K tokens) along with up to 1,000 independent questions. Instead of generating answer text token by token, the model scores candidate options in a single forward pass and returns probabilities and confidence scores in 150-250 ms end-to-end latency, priced at $0.10 per 1M input tokens with zero output tokens billed. Google says the model leads across benchmarks for agent routing, reasoning, ranking, and multimodal understanding, with the lowest calibration error, and is nearly 5x cheaper than Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite with the Interactions API across a shared benchmark suite. Teams across Google are already using it, including Gemini Notebook for real-time model routing, Stitch for rapid design iteration, CC for emoji selection, and Google Search as a safety layer for MCP tools.

05 Hacker News

Retirement Savings Are Fueling the AI Bubble

Tech stocks lost $3.2 trillion in a single day last Thursday, and the week before, the "Magnificent Seven" tech companies lost nearly a trillion dollars after earnings reports showed Google, Amazon, and Meta spending heavily on AI and relying more on borrowing. The article argues that ordinary workers' retirement savings are helping fund this AI boom: public pension funds have put money into private credit and equity funds, and the Trump administration has made the $15 trillion in private sector retirement accounts available to private funds for the first time. According to Americans for Financial Reform, private equity firms are behind 25-30% of data center financing, and savers are exposed to risks these companies do not have to disclose. S&P has said the credit quality of big tech companies is weakening, and credit default swap trading linked to their bonds is rising, which reminds observers of 2008. The article says pension trustees and employees can push to keep retirement money out of these risky, unregulated assets.

AI, activity, and outcomes in software engineering

A UC Irvine software engineering seminar talk on October 30, 2026, asks why software teams aren't shipping faster despite the spread of AI tools. The talk draws on several 2026 papers, including one by Cha, Badea, Baym, Bird, Butler, Murphy-Hill and Hadley called "The Activity-Outcome Gap: Why AI Does Not Solve Software Engineering," based on 30 interviews. Another cited paper, NBER Working Paper 35275 by Demirer, Musolff and Yang, compares productivity effects across generations of AI coding tools under the title "Writing Code vs. Shipping Code." Other referenced work looks at what AI has let developers unlock, whether code review agents can detect bugs in a "lights-off" software factory, where developers draw the line on AI autonomy, and what drives individual and team use of generative AI tools. The talk matters because it examines the gap between more AI-driven activity and actual delivery outcomes in software engineering.

10 The Verge

Google’s AlphaGenome 2 zooms in on the cellular roots of disease

Google DeepMind has released AlphaGenome 2, a new AI model that predicts how genetic changes affect different types of cells. It is the successor to AlphaGenome, which came out last year, and was trained on more than 100 million individual cell profiles across 68 tissues. Google says the model identifies nearly twice as many variants that can change gene expression in cells as its previous best model, and it can make predictions about new cell types without retraining. The work matters because the same genetic variant can have very different effects depending on the cell type and state, so a finer-grained view could help scientists connect variants to disease and find drug targets. Google is making the model available for non-commercial research through its AlphaGenome API, with a limited private preview for commercial research.

Big Walk used AI to fix a bug. Now the indie gaming world is having a reckoning.

House House, the developer of the indie game Big Walk, has admitted that it used large language models sparingly near the end of development to help fix technical problems, including a Unity engine bug that caused slow performance from "infinite length" shadows. The disclosure came after 19 members of the Independent Games Festival Awards jury signed a statement saying they would decline to vote for any game that uses generative AI in any capacity, citing ecological, economic, psychological, and labor harms. House House says the AI-assisted code represents well under 0.1% of Big Walk's code and that the suggested fix had to be modified extensively. The controversy matters because it has pushed the indie game community into a public debate over how AI tools are used in development and whether that use should be disclosed to players.
