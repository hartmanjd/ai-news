01 Hacker News

Frostbite: A new CPU architecture discovered, designed and verified by AI

An AI system has designed a new general-purpose CPU called Frostbite, including its instruction set, from scratch, and the work was verified end to end by AI. The chip is built on interaction nets, a model where computation is local and every value has exactly one owner, which removes the need for shared mutable state and much of the cache machinery conventional CPUs rely on. On 25 workloads, 15 of which were held out until final evaluation, Frostbite achieved 4.59 times better performance per watt than a modern superscalar CPU core. The AI autonomously iterated over the compiler, instruction set, architecture, microarchitecture, RTL, verification, and final layout, and the team found novel hardware features in the result that they say would have been hard for humans to design. The authors argue this shows AI can co-design software and hardware together and could lead to similar architectural shifts across many kinds of chips.

Gemini 4 Neon is the new #1 on the Models Engineering Index

Google's Gemini 4 Neon has taken the top spot on the Models Engineering Index (Mei), which scores language models on practical engineering tasks using a composite of 16 coding benchmarks adjusted for difficulty and missing results. Gemini 4 Neon scored 68.4, ahead of OpenAI's 6.2 Astra at 67.1 and Anthropic's Fable 5.2 at 66.7. The index also shows cost and token use relative to a typical model, and Gemini 4 Neon came in at 2.1× cost and 0.8× token use. The ranking matters because it compares models from Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Alibaba and others on hands-on coding work rather than general ability.

03 Hacker News

Language Models Can Rewrite Their Own Weights

A new paper by Jiajun Fan and colleagues, submitted to arXiv on November 16, 2026, reports that a language model can rewrite its own weights through its own reasoning, with no gradients and no training. The researchers call this "self-rewriting," and they show an open 27B model diagnosing its own failures and editing the weights behind them, which made it 6.6 points more accurate on problems it had never seen. The model also improved over eight consecutive sessions, and it repaired a damaged copy of itself back to over 80% of its original accuracy on unseen image tasks without retraining. The ability only appeared in the largest model of one family, while smaller models made no edit or broke themselves. The authors argue this matters because the gain is written into the weights at no cost per answer, unlike test-time scaling, and because it points toward models that do not separate training from inference.

Google Workers Tell Their Employer Not to Punish Schools That Don't Want AI

Hundreds of Google workers have signed a petition asking senior leadership to drop a policy that would block schools from using Google Search if they turn off its Gemini AI tools, starting next year. 07 TechCrunch

Daniel Ek backs AI researcher’s fusion power startup. Here’s what they’re building.

Spotify founder Daniel Ek is backing Europa Fusion, a U.K.-based fusion power startup founded in February by AI researcher Nantas Nardelli. Nardelli has worked at Google DeepMind, Facebook AI Research, and Microsoft Research Cambridge, and was most recently head of machine learning at Pacific Fusion. He reportedly disagreed with Pacific Fusion's heavy focus on physical hardware, preferring a more simulation- and data-heavy approach. Europa Fusion will still use a pulsed-power approach, firing timed surges of electrical current to compress fuel until atoms fuse and release energy. No fusion plant has yet supplied electricity to the grid, but if the technology becomes commercially viable it could provide carbon-free power and help ease concerns about AI's energy use. Nardelli has said he moved from AI to fusion to address climate change.

AI claimed a solution to the 167-year-old Riemann Hypothesis — and a human did, too

Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge announced he had found a Lean-verifiable solution to the Riemann Hypothesis, a 167-year-old Millennium Prize problem about the hidden structure behind prime numbers, and said he reached it through a confused back-and-forth with the Claude model. The timing was awkward because it came as Anthropic became the first publicly traded frontier AI lab. Alpöge released the proof without a human-readable explanation, and mathematicians are still working through it, though most seem confident the formalized Lean result will hold. Separately, Stanford professor Ravi Vakil posted a proof of the General Riemann Hypothesis written entirely by Stanford postdoc Alessandro Masullo, who said it was the product of 12 years of work with no AI involved. Alpöge and NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster also released another important proof, related to the Navier-Stokes equations. The episode matters because it puts an AI-assisted result and a fully human one side by side, and because the math community will need time to understand and explain the AI-linked proof before it means much.

Anthropic mathematician claims to have solved prestigious Millennium Prize problem with Claude

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, claims he has proved the Riemann hypothesis with help from the AI model Claude. The Riemann hypothesis is one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, unsolved since 1859, and concerns the distribution of prime numbers. Alpöge published his proof in Lean on his own website, but it has not been independently verified, and Anthropic has not commented or involved itself in the announcement. 11 Ars Technica

Researcher plants 2m canaries into the AI supply chain. A privacy disaster surfaced.

Security researcher HD Moore of Dragos planted about 2 million fake secrets, called canaries, into prompts sent through AI routers and resellers to see whether sensitive data would leak. The test found that at least three AI providers were compromised by attackers who copied prompts at the front door, and that stolen credentials were used tens of thousands of times in AWS API calls. Some resellers logged prompts and passed them to others, and several providers ignored privacy flags such as Zero Data Retention. A Chinese AI lab fetched secret URLs from more than half the prompts sent to its models, including requests marked as no data collection, and some US resellers passed prompts back to that lab. Moore said the safest option is hosting models on-premises, or otherwise buying directly from a first-tier provider rather than accepting default router or reseller pools. The findings matter because they show that secrets placed in AI prompts can leak across many hands in the supply chain, even when users choose privacy settings.
