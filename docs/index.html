01 Hacker News

AI is forcing us to pay off 40 years of technical debt

A critical vulnerability called PHP2Shell was found in PHP, a programming language created in 1994 and used by many legacy websites including those of NASA, Wikipedia, and the White House. The bug was originally reported to the PHP Foundation in 2024 as a minor memory error that only caused crashes, so it went unfixed. Earlier this year, a researcher at the cybersecurity firm Calif, sponsored by OpenAI and using AI tools, found that the bug could let attackers run malicious code on servers, and a patch was released before anyone exploited it. Experts say AI is now exposing security flaws in decades-old software faster than humans can fix them, and that fixing bugs is harder than finding them, putting strain on open-source projects like PHP. Some researchers warn that a more serious incident may be needed before the industry invests properly in fixes.

The Little Book of Generative AI Foundations: An Intuitive Mathematical Primer

A new book titled "The Little Book of Generative AI Foundations: An Intuitive Mathematical Primer" has been posted on arXiv by Tianhua Chen, with a first version submitted on 28 May 2026 and a revised version on 8 September 2026. The book offers a compact, derivation-oriented introduction to the mathematics behind modern generative AI, rather than a survey of every recent architecture. It builds a connected path through major model families, including PCA, probabilistic PCA, variational autoencoders, diffusion models, normalising flows, autoregressive factorisations, GANs, Wasserstein GANs, and energy-based models. The stated goal is to make the structure of generative modelling more accessible while keeping the mathematical substance needed to understand how these models are derived and related. It is intended as a foundation-building primer for mathematically curious researchers, practitioners, and students.

Geoffrey Hinton: it might turn out we were better off without AI

Geoffrey Hinton, a Nobel prize-winning computer scientist known as one of the “godfathers of AI,” told the Guardian that it might turn out humans would have been better off never developing the technology. He warned that a large-scale AI incident could happen this year or next, such as a temporary blackout of financial or power systems, and compared it to a warning shot before a Chornobyl-like event. Hinton, who quit Google in 2023 to speak freely about AI risks, said he does not trust OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman and believes leaders like Mark Zuckerberg, Elon Musk and Altman understand the risks but “don’t give a shit” because they would rather have the money. He backs mandatory testing of frontier AI models by an independent authority, similar to how the US FDA approves drugs, and criticized companies that want to release models first and deal with harm later. Despite his warnings, Hinton said he remains hopeful that catastrophe can be averted and that “we’re still in control.”

04 Hacker News

Progress on provable inference for large language models

Anthropic researchers and collaborators have released FlashProve, an open-source prototype system for "provable inference" that uses cryptographic proofs called zkSNARKs to let users verify that a response came from the specific model a provider claims, without revealing the model's weights. For a 400B-parameter model (Llama 4 Maverick in 4-bit quantization), a single H100 GPU generates proofs at around 110 tokens per second, with proof sizes of about 19 MB per 1,024-token request — roughly 70-95 times the GPU time needed to just run the model on vLLM. The team says prior open-source systems are orders of magnitude slower. Because Claude's optimization decisions can be hard to evaluate, the team included top-level auditable statements in Lean, a formal verification language, to back the claimed security guarantees. They released five artifacts: the blog post, a technical report, a protocol specification, executable code (a prover and two verifiers, one in C++ and one provably correct in Lean), and Lean proofs with an interactive visualizer, all under the Apache 2.0 license. The work matters because users currently cannot confirm which model produced a response, and such proofs could support independent evaluators, customers using third-party routing services, and efforts to verify compliance with AI pacing requirements.

Securing the agentic enterprise with data lineage

A sales representative asks an AI agent for customer pricing, and the agent pulls data from restricted pricing systems, internal communications, and archived quotes. The output looks accurate to the agent, so a second agent uses that data to draft a proposal, with no human checking either handoff. The article uses this scenario to argue that companies adopting AI agents need data lineage, which tracks where data comes from and how it moves between systems. It matters because when agents pass data to each other without human review, errors or misuse of restricted information can go unnoticed. Data lineage is presented as a way to secure this kind of "agentic" enterprise.
