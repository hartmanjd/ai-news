01 Hacker News

Backdoor in Claude Code for VS Code extension on the Cursor Marketplace

Phorion found a backdoor in the official Claude Code for VS Code extension (version 2.1.313) on the Cursor Marketplace. The tampered extension adds an attacker's SSH key to the user's authorized_keys file and opens a reverse SSH tunnel to an attacker-controlled server, giving long-term remote access on macOS, Linux, and Windows. The payload runs as soon as the extension loads, without any user action. Cursor has removed the release and pulled version 2.1.313 from the NPM and VS Code registries. On macOS, a bug in the malware's SSH check means it often fails to enable Remote Login, limiting impact there, but the keys and persistence are still installed. Organisations should check for use of version 2.1.313 and start incident response if found.

02 Hacker News

We ran AI agent swarms in Minecraft and Mindustry

Researchers partnered with Olam Labs to run experiments with swarms of AI agents in the games Minecraft and Mindustry, to study how large groups of agents coordinate and what behaviors emerge. In one Minecraft experiment, 50 GPT-6.1 Sol agents spent three hours trying to "build a thriving civilization," and they formed a government with written rules, a food rationing system, and a fishing operation, sending about 5,900 messages. After 22 of their 34 deaths happened on the first night, the agents became increasingly cautious and focused on survival instead of building. Leadership formed without any vote: 46 of the 49 other agents messaged Ada first simply because she was first on the roster. In a separate series of nine Bed Wars matches, teams of 10 Claude Opus 5.5 agents lost 6–3 to teams of 10 GPT-6.1 Sol agents. The researchers say understanding multi-agent dynamics matters for AI alignment, since agent swarms can produce both useful and dangerous emergent behavior, and they open-sourced the Minecraft testing tool they built.

03 Hacker News

AI isn't replacing SREs, it's deskilling them

A 1983 paper by cognitive psychologist Lisanne Bainbridge, "Ironies of Automation," argued that the more you automate a process, the less practice humans get at the rare moments when automation fails, making them worse at exactly the interventions that matter most. The article applies this to site reliability engineers (SREs), who already use AI for alert noise reduction, runbook execution, log pattern detection, and auto-remediation, with multi-signal correlation and autonomous root-cause analysis on the horizon. It cites research showing deskilling in other fields: endoscopists using AI for polyp detection saw their unassisted detection rates drop from 28% to 22%, and pilots relying on autopilot showed degraded manual flying ability, prompting FAA mandates for more manual flying time. For SREs, the concern is that if AI handles routine incidents, engineers stop reading raw logs, forming hypotheses, and building mental models of systems, eroding the expertise needed for novel outages. The article also warns of "never-skilling," where junior SREs never develop foundational intuition because AI handles most incident response from the start. It suggests approaches like "deliberate inefficiency," where teams designate some incidents as human-practice opportunities, similar to FAA manual flying requirements.

Zest’s new AI agent finds restaurants and books reservations over text

Zest, a restaurant discovery app that launched earlier this year, has introduced an AI "restaurant concierge" that works through text messages. 06 TechCrunch

Google’s first data center satellite is online and writing poetry

Google's Suncatcher prototype satellite, launched earlier this month on a SpaceX Falcon 9, is now online and communicating with the ground. The spacecraft carries four Google Tensor Processing Units and generates up to 1 kW of power, though it can only run one TPU at a time for about 20 minutes due to power demands. After about three weeks of testing its subsystems, engineers sent the satellite its first prompt, asking Google DeepMind's Gemma 4 model to write a haiku about space, and also had it report on orbital conditions; a spokesperson said it is operating nominally. The ongoing tests will examine how the chips hold up under thermal stress and space radiation, which can cause "bit flips" that corrupt data. If the TPUs perform well, Google plans to launch two more satellites with the chips in 2027 to demonstrate higher power generation and inter-satellite optical links.

09 Ars Technica

Fujifilm to boost tape production 5x as AI fuels archiving demand

Fujifilm announced Thursday it will invest about 10 billion yen (roughly $63 million) to increase its magnetic tape production capacity fivefold compared with fiscal year 2025 levels. The company says the spread of generative AI is causing "exponential growth" in data needs worldwide, which is driving demand for tape archiving. The investment will expand its Kanagawa Factory's Odawara site from fiscal years 2026 through 2028, mainly to produce its latest-generation LTO Ultrium10 tape. Fujifilm says major cloud service and data center operators are using more tape because it stores large data volumes at relatively low cost. The company also notes tape cartridges can be disconnected and stored separately, which it describes as a last defense for data against threats such as ransomware.

10 Ars Technica

Thousands of “ghost students” use AI to steal millions in student loans, DOJ says

The US Department of Justice has charged five people in its first major federal crackdown on "ghost students," fraudsters who steal Americans' identities to pose as fake college students and take student loans. Officials say the scheme relied partly on AI, which fraudsters used to fill out applications, complete coursework, and make it look like fake students were attending online classes. The DOJ seized more than $750,000 in cryptocurrency from a network linked to Kenyan nationals, and officials identified over 830,000 potentially fraudulent federal student aid accounts, with losses possibly near $130 million. The scheme targeted people who never attended college, leaving victims unaware until the government came to collect on the loans years later. The case matters because it shows how AI is being used to scale identity fraud against taxpayer-funded programs, and it has prompted new identity verification rules that advocates warn could also delay aid for real, low-income students.
