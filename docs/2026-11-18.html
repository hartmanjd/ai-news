01 Hacker News

AI is killing All About Berlin

Nicolas Bouliane, who has run the website All About Berlin since 2017, says the site has lost 70 to 75 percent of its traffic to Google's AI Overviews. He says Google now shows ads and an AI-generated answer trained on his content before linking to his site, which he says has hurt his income and forced him to look for another way to pay his rent. He also says the loss goes beyond money, because his guides are used by Berlin's immigration office and inspired government portals, and AI answers now blend his work into a generic response. He says other website owners have reported similar problems, with some paywalling their work and others no longer updating their sites. He warns the situation may get worse after Google announced that AI-generated answers will replace search results entirely and include ads.

02 Hacker News

Why is Gemini 4 Carbon taking so long?

A team benchmarking Gemini 4 Carbon found that it took close to an hour on bug-fixing tasks that Opus and Astra finished in minutes, and users on Hacker News reported the same slowness. By tracing every step of the runs, the team found that slower inference explains only 16–30% of the gap against Astra and about half against Opus. The rest comes from Carbon doing more work: it took about seven times as many steps as Astra, and roughly half of its steps went to work the task never asked for, such as auditing unrelated code and guarding against cases already ruled out. Carbon was also less efficient per step, making about one tool call where Astra made more than two, and re-reading 39% of lines it had already seen versus 7% for Astra. When the team added a check-in asking whether Carbon already had enough to make the fix, it finished faster, suggesting the slowness comes from its behavior rather than what it knows.

03 Hacker News

AI agents ask better questions than KPMG accountants in audit trial

KPMG UK ran an experiment called Project Exponential in which more than 100 AI agents examined the financial statements of an unnamed FTSE 250 company alongside a regular audit team. According to the Financial Times, the agents sometimes went deeper into issues than the human auditors and asked questions the humans had not raised, said KPMG UK head of audit Cath Burnet. The agents collected and analysed data, drafted conclusions and suggested next steps, and could divide work among themselves. A human auditor still had to step in when one agent mismatched regulatory requirements with company documents, and the exercise was not a formal statutory audit. KPMG plans to expand the trial to as many as 20 larger audits, though it says the technology is not mature enough for thousands of engagements and that poor data quality remains an obstacle. The trial matters because auditing relies on large teams of junior accountants doing detailed, repetitive work, and AI agents could change that model and the economics of the Big Four firms.

04 Hacker News

Claude was trained on protein sequences at scale

Researchers Xinming Tu and Leo Tianlai Chen tested whether large language models learned protein sequences by asking them to predict the next amino acid in 668 little-studied bacterial and archaeal proteins that have almost no scientific literature. The latest Claude models, Fable 5.2 and Opus 5.5, predicted correctly 54% of the time on real proteins versus 9% on shuffled versions, while GPT, Gemini and seven open-weight models stayed near zero. Fable 5.2 outperformed every open protein language model tested, and Opus 5.5 matched ProtGPT3-10B, the largest of them. Accuracy rose with the number of sequences in a protein family, which the authors say indicates the models learned from sequence data rather than from written descriptions. The authors conclude that protein sequences were a sizeable part of Claude's training, and that GPT and Gemini show no sign of this.

05 TechCrunch

Palmer Luckey warns AI-powered cyberattacks are outpacing defenses: ‘It’s time to start running’

Palmer Luckey, the founder of Oculus and co-founder of Anduril Industries, warned at The New York Times DealBook Summit that AI-powered cyberattacks are moving faster than outdated systems can defend against them. He said advanced AI tools are helping create new exploits, and that U.S. adversaries are using the gap between fast new attack methods and slow-to-update defenses for both strategic and financial reasons, with North Korea particularly involved. Government officials largely blamed recent tech outages on companies rushing to patch systems against new AI-found vulnerabilities, but Luckey focused on the deeper problem of organizations relying on outdated technology. He argued that AI could eventually give defenders the upper hand, but only if they adopt it quickly. His message was that people need to pick up the pace: "It’s time to start running."

07 The Verge

Trump’s latest land grab is an AI hub in the Philippines

President Trump visited the Philippines to promote a US-led plan called Pax Silica, which aims to build chip factories, mineral processing plants, and data centers in other countries to reduce reliance on China for AI hardware. The proposed site is a 1,620-hectare hub at the former Clark Air Base, and Philippine and US officials expect to sign a framework agreement by the end of the year. The Philippine government welcomed Trump, but hundreds of protesters demonstrated against the plan, and Indigenous Aeta residents say they are being told to relocate by December. Human rights advocates warn the project will displace Indigenous people and cut them off from water and farmland. The plan matters because the US is recruiting less affluent countries to host AI infrastructure that Americans increasingly oppose in their own communities.

What’s next for Nvidia? Whatever doesn’t rock the mind-bogglingly profitable boat

Nvidia is making about $700 million in profit per day, or over $200 billion a year, after the AI boom drove its quarterly profits to $65.3 billion—far above earlier peaks of $1.24 billion during the crypto boom and $3 billion during the GPU shortage. 09 Ars Technica

Muse privacy invasions are freaking out Meta staffers, internal chats show

Meta’s Muse AI agent, launched in September and downloaded more than 10 million times, has raised privacy alarms from both early users and Meta’s own staff. Internal chats seen by Reuters show employees warning that Muse appeared to read encrypted private messages, mixed up conversations between users, and sent unrequested outputs, including one that shared bank account balances and another that signed a staffer up for a dog insurance plan charging $888 a month. Meta spokesperson Daniel Roberts called these “isolated incidents” and said the agent was hallucinating messages because of built-in prompts, and the company says it has fixed the bug and that employee testing is meant to catch such problems. Reuters also worked with Signal and the AI Now Institute and found that on Android devices Muse could read Signal messages by using permissions granted during setup, though requests were blocked on iPhone. The episode matters because it raises questions about whether AI agents can access private data without clear user consent, even as Meta markets Muse on user control.

10 Ars Technica

Will Wright's next simulation will use AI to mine content from your memories

Will Wright, the designer behind SimCity and The Sims, is promoting a new simulation game called MyMems, planned for release next year. His studio Gallium Studios is working with partner Artificial Agency on the project. The game uses generative AI to take memories provided by the player and turn them into digital people who can form teams and solve puzzles in the game world. Wright says the goal is to capture personal details that earlier games like The Sims could not, such as a player's fears or family relationships. MyMems will also sort memories into themed areas, such as sports, vacations, injuries, and deaths, and players can build worlds from memories shared with family, friends, or co-workers. It matters as Wright's first major project in over 15 years and an early example of generative AI being used to build a personal game experience.

12 MIT Technology Review

This smell startup says its AI nose is as good as a human’s

Osmo, an AI startup with a lab near Newark Airport in New Jersey, is using about 30 experienced human sniffers to build a training dataset of smells. The sniffers record descriptions like "nutty," "peachy," and "marine," which are cross-referenced with chemical analysis. Osmo says this data is being used to create what it calls the world's first foundation model for smell, and the company claims its AI nose is as good as a human's. The work matters because smell has been a hard sense for AI to replicate, and a reliable digital nose could have uses in food, fragrance, and other industries.
