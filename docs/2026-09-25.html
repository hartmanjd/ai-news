U.S. appeals court upholds designation of Anthropic as supply chain risk

A federal appeals court in Washington, D.C., ruled 2-1 on Friday to uphold the Pentagon's designation of Anthropic as a supply chain risk, rejecting the company's claims that the ban was arbitrary, unauthorized, and unconstitutional. The Defense Department labeled Anthropic a supply chain risk in March after negotiations broke down over how the military could use its Claude AI models, with the Pentagon wanting unrestricted access and Anthropic seeking limits on autonomous weapons and mass surveillance. The designation blocks the U.S. military and its contractors from using Anthropic's models. A San Francisco federal judge had already ruled a separate but related designation illegal last month, and Anthropic says it disagrees with Friday's decision and is considering further legal options. The court delayed the ruling from taking effect to allow Anthropic to seek a rehearing or appeal to the Supreme Court.

Classified Estimates Show the NSA Is Paying Billions to Test AI Models

The National Security Agency told lawmakers it is spending billions of dollars in taxpayer funds this year to test and evaluate advanced AI models, according to two sources familiar with classified estimates. The NSA's Artificial Intelligence Security Center began testing frontier models to find national security vulnerabilities after several high-profile hacking and security incidents. This cost is far higher than earlier government estimates, such as a Congressional Budget Office estimate that a bipartisan House AI oversight bill would cost about $20 million per year. The spending has led lawmakers to believe a broader AI regulatory system could cost tens of billions of dollars annually, and it may increase calls for AI companies to help pay for reviewing their own models. President Trump has mostly resisted federal AI oversight, while some tech leaders and AI safety experts disagree on whether companies should police themselves or face government-run reviews.

03 Hacker News

Jevmem – automatic project memory for Claude Code, built on Jev

Jevmem is a new tool that gives Claude Code automatic project memory by saving decisions, constraints, bugs, and todos from chats into a file called JEVMEM.md. It works as a Claude Code plugin or via npm, and also supports Cursor and Codex, though capture and recall are only fully automatic for Claude Code. When a decision changes, the old line is marked superseded rather than deleted, and relevant lines are added to Claude's context in later sessions. In a test on 66 held-out turns, jevmem's median decision took 0.30 seconds versus 2.8–4.3 seconds for six current LLMs, with 98.5% save/skip accuracy and 95.5% save+kind accuracy, within the LLMs' range. It matters because it offers a fast, cheap way to decide what to remember on every message, though the author notes it is not the most accurate option and the eval sets are not independent benchmarks.

04 Hacker News

Uproar in France over award-winning author accused of using AI

A Canadian-Haitian author, Thélyson Orélien, has been accused of using artificial intelligence to write his first novel, C'était ça ou mourir, shortly after winning the Prix du Roman Fnac and becoming a finalist for the Goncourt and Renaudot prizes. A little-known group called Balance ton Claude claimed the book was "almost totally" written by AI, based on a detection programme called Pangram. Orélien denied the accusation, saying he wrote the book himself and that the first draft dates from 2019, before text-generating AI was widely available, and his publisher Grasset defended him. The case matters because it echoes a recent controversy over another writer's prize and adds to the debate over how reliable AI-detection tools are, since critics say they can wrongly flag human writing, especially from non-European dialects.

08 The Verge

Sony and UMG are suing Suno again

Sony and Universal Music Group have filed another lawsuit against the AI music company Suno. The labels say Suno's new v6 model still infringes their copyrights because it was trained on outputs from earlier Suno models, which were themselves trained on unlicensed music taken from YouTube and other sources. Sony and UMG call this "model laundering," arguing that training a new model on an infringing model's outputs does not remove the infringement. Suno has said v6 was trained from the ground up with a new set of data, including user creations, but has not given more details. Sony and UMG are among the labels that have not signed a licensing deal with Suno. The case matters because it tests whether AI music companies can escape copyright claims by building new models on the outputs of older, allegedly infringing ones.

09 Ars Technica

Microsoft stops insisting you need a "Copilot+ PC"

Microsoft has stopped using the "Copilot+ PC" label on its newest Surface computers, even though those devices still meet the requirements for it. The new Surface Pro 12-inch and Surface Laptop 13-inch, launching October 13, run Qualcomm Snapdragon X2 Plus processors with an 80 TOPS NPU, above the 40 TOPS minimum for Copilot+ PCs. Brett Ostrum, a Microsoft Surface vice president, told Windows Central the new computers "are not called Copilot+ PCs" despite meeting the old bar. Qualcomm's Kedar Kondap said the point of the label was to define a category of devices with a certain NPU performance level, and that the same experiences can now be offered without the term. Microsoft's Copilot+ PC landing page now redirects to a "performance PCs" page that mentions the label far less prominently. This matters because it signals Microsoft is backing away from the branding it introduced in 2024 to help buyers identify Windows systems built for local AI workloads.

10 Ars Technica

Trump admin using AI to deny medical care for seniors in disastrous experiment

The Trump administration launched a pilot program in January called WISeR that uses artificial intelligence to approve or deny prior authorization requests for certain Medicare services in six states, with plans to run until 2031. The program has been plagued by technical problems, long delays, and high denial rates, according to federal documents obtained by the Electronic Frontier Foundation. One vendor, Virtix, denied 53 percent of requests it reviewed and was placed on a corrective action plan for missing the 72-hour decision window. Healthcare providers reported patients crying in pain while waiting weeks or months for decisions, with one calling the program "a disgrace to the human race." The Government Accountability Office found in May that Trump officials did not follow proper procedure in setting up the program, questioning its legality, but it continues to move forward.

11 MIT Technology Review

The Pentagon wants $30 million to build an AI-powered lie detector

The Pentagon has requested $30.3 million over five years for a program called Polygraph+ or Polygraph Next, which would use AI, machine learning, and “standoff sensing” to improve lie detection for vetting employees and detecting insider threats. The program would be run by the Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency and comes as the Pentagon under Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has increasingly used polygraph tests to hunt for leakers. Experts criticized the plan, with one legal scholar calling AI-enabled lie detection “the worst of both worlds” because it adds uncertainty to a technology whose validity is already disputed. Polygraph results are rarely admissible in court, and a 2003 National Research Council report called the evidence for their effectiveness “weak at best.” Researchers say there is no single reliable sign of lying, and past attempts to build new lie detection technologies have failed to produce dependable results outside the lab.
